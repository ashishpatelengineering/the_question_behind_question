Part One

Moving Data

Every question Uddhav will be asked is, underneath, the same question: how do you get data from where it is to where it is needed, and why that way. He knows how to build it. He is about to learn how to say why.

Chapter One

The Question He Could Not Answer

Where the real test hides

The Idea Nobody is asking what you know. They are asking how you decide.

The interview ran for fifty minutes, and Uddhav knew it was over after eleven.

It had started well enough. The interviewer was a woman called Radhika, from the data platform team, and she began with easy questions about things he had built. Uddhav answered those comfortably. He had been a data engineer for three years. He had built real pipelines that ran every night and did not fall over.

Then she asked the question that actually mattered.

"Orders sit in the application database," she said. "The analytics team wants to report on them. Design me the pipeline."

Uddhav relaxed, because he had built this exact thing twice before and knew the answer without having to think about it.

"I would use ELT," he said. "Extract the data, load it into the warehouse as it is, and do the transformations inside the warehouse."

"Good," said Radhika. "Why?"

And nothing came.

He knew ELT was right. He knew it the way he knew his own address. But nobody had ever asked him to explain why it was right, and now that somebody had, there was nothing in his head at all.

"Because it is the modern approach," he said. "It is what most teams do now."

Radhika waited a moment, in case more was coming. Nothing more came. She nodded, made a small note, and moved on to the next question, and the call went on politely for another forty minutes.

It was the note he kept thinking about afterwards. Two days later the rejection email arrived.

He told Keshav about it on Thursday, on the steps outside the chai stall near Keshav's office. It was the hour when the office buildings empty, and the road below them was solid with scooters and impatient horns. The stall's kettle hissed behind them. The man who ran it wiped the same six inches of counter over and over, the way he did every evening.

The two of them had started at the same company years ago, at neighbouring desks. Keshav worked somewhere bigger now, and part of his job was sitting on the other side of interviews like this one. He claimed to have done two hundred of them. Uddhav had always privately estimated ninety.

"Radhika asked me why," Uddhav said, "and I told her it was the modern approach."

Keshav looked up. "Radhika who?"

"Radhika Iyer. Data platform."

Keshav set his cup down on the step very carefully, and took slightly longer over it than the cup required.

"I know her," he said. "We worked together for two years."

"Is that going to be awkward?"

"Not for me." He picked the cup back up. "The modern approach."

"Do not start."

"I am not saying anything at all," said Keshav, who was grinning. "It is very modern. Extremely current."

Uddhav stood up to leave. Keshav caught his sleeve and pulled him back down onto the step.

"Sit down. You have not finished your chai, and anyway your answer was correct."

That stopped him properly. "It was correct?"

"ELT is the right default for that situation. If she had only wanted the answer, you passed in the first eleven minutes."

"Then why did I lose?"

"Because she was not asking the question you thought she was asking." Keshav turned to face him. "She already knows how to build a pipeline. She has built a hundred of them. What she cannot see from your resume is whether you can think. So she asks something with a known answer, and then she listens for what is holding your answer up."

Uddhav thought about Radhika making her small note, and felt slightly sick.

"There was nothing holding mine up," he said.

"There was nothing holding yours up."

"All right," said Uddhav. "Then tell me the actual words. What I should have said instead."

Keshav pulled the napkin out from under his cup, turned it over, and wrote three words down the side of it. 

choice   what you would do
reason   why it suits this situation
flip     what would make you do the opposite

"You gave her the first one and then you stopped," he said. "One part out of three. On its own, that sounds like a preference." He tapped the second line. "Add this and it becomes a reason." He tapped the third. "Add this and it becomes judgement, which is the thing she was actually shopping for."

"Say the whole thing properly. The way I should have said it."

Keshav said it flatly, without any performance at all.

"I would use ELT here, because we keep the raw orders and can rebuild the reports later if the logic changes. If the source held data we were not allowed to store, I would transform first instead."

Uddhav read the napkin twice. It was three short lines, and it was the difference between the job and the email.

"That is the whole difference?"

"That is the whole difference. It is the same decision you made, about twelve seconds longer."

Uddhav took out his phone and typed the three words into it. Keshav watched him do it and started laughing.

"You wrote them on a napkin," said Uddhav. "Like a spy."

What he said I would use ELT, because that is the modern approach.
What it needed I would use ELT here, because we keep the raw orders and can rebuild the reports later if the logic changes. If the source held data we were not allowed to store, I would transform first instead.

"Here is the part that should cheer you up," said Keshav. "You did not lose because you lack knowledge. You have plenty of knowledge. You lost because nobody has ever made you say your reasons out loud, so you never noticed that you did not have any."

"That is not cheering me up."

"It should. Missing knowledge takes years to fix. This takes about six weeks." Keshav drained the last of his chai and stood up. "The reasons are already sitting inside those four pipelines you built. You built them on instinct and never dug them out. So we dig them out. Thursdays."

"Thursdays?"

"You bring me the questions you froze on, and I ask them again until you stop freezing."

Uddhav started to say something about not wanting to be a burden. He managed about four words of it.

"Do not," said Keshav, already halfway down the steps. "I watch good engineers lose to this every single week, and I have to sit there and say nothing, because I am the one holding the clipboard. Let me fix one of you."

He turned back before he reached the road.

"And bring samosas. I am not doing this hungry."

The question, and how to hold it

"Design the pipeline that moves order data from the application database into the analytics warehouse."

Do not begin with a tool. Begin with size and rate: how much data, how often it changes, how fresh the reports need to be. Then give the three parts. Choice, reason, and the case where it flips. Everything in this book is practice at those three.

What Uddhav Learned

Interviewers are not collecting answers. They already have the answers. They are looking for the reasoning that produced one, because reasoning is the part that carries over to problems they have not thought of yet.

So a complete answer has three parts. The choice. The reason it suits this particular situation. And the conditions that would make you choose differently. An answer missing the third part sounds like a preference. An answer with all three sounds like judgement.

The most common failure is not ignorance. It is knowing the right thing for reasons you have never said out loud.

They are not asking what you know. They are asking how you decide.

Chapter Two

Before or After

ETL, ELT, and the word "modern"

The Idea There is only one real decision here. Do you clean the data before you store it, or after?

Keshav lived on the third floor of a building with no lift, which he mentioned every single time anyone visited, as though the stairs were a personal injustice done to him by the landlord.

The stairwell smelled of somebody's dinner, mustard seeds cracking in hot oil two floors up. A bicycle was chained to the railing on the second landing, where it had clearly been chained for years. Uddhav arrived at eight with a bag of samosas, because he had decided that if somebody was going to spend their evenings fixing him, the least he could do was feed them.

Inside, the flat was one large room that did everything. A low table with two cushions on the floor beside it. A ceiling fan that wobbled slightly on its highest setting, which was the only setting Keshav ever used. A window open to the sound of the main road four streets away.

"Sit," said Keshav. "Eat one of your own samosas. And then I am going to ask you the same question that woman asked you, and you are going to answer it badly again, and then we will fix it."

"That sounds like a wonderful evening."

"It will be. Right." Keshav sat back and put on a face that was clearly meant to be an interviewer. "Orders sit in the application database. Analytics needs to report on them. Design the pipeline."

Uddhav opened his mouth, and stopped.

"I was about to say ELT."

"I know. Say it. It is the right answer."

"But I still cannot tell you why."

"Then we start further back than you think." Keshav pulled his notebook over. "Forget both words for a minute. Any pipeline that moves data does three jobs, and only three. It takes the data out of the source. It changes the data into a useful shape. And it puts the data somewhere it can be read."

"Extract, transform, load," said Uddhav.

"Those three, yes. And here is the entire argument of the last fifteen years." Keshav held up two fingers. "You have to do all three jobs. The only question is what order you do them in. That is it. That is the whole thing."

Uddhav looked at him.

"That cannot be the whole thing."

"It genuinely is. Watch." He drew two lines on the page, one under the other. 

ETL, the older order
source DB → [extract] → staging server → [transform] → [load] → warehouse

ELT, the newer order
source DB → [extract] → [load] → lake or warehouse → [transform in place]

"Look at the middle of the first line," Keshav said. "There is a machine there. A staging server. That is a real computer that somebody buys and runs and patches, sitting between the source and the warehouse, and its whole job is to do the cleaning before anything is allowed through."

"And in the second line there is nothing in the middle."

"There is nothing in the middle. The data goes straight into the warehouse, and then the warehouse does the cleaning itself, using its own power. That missing machine is worth remembering, because it comes back later when we talk about why the industry changed its mind."

"In the first one," Keshav said, "you clean the data on the way in. There is usually a separate machine sitting in the middle doing that work. By the time anything reaches the warehouse, it is already tidy. The warehouse never sees a messy row in its life."

"And the second one?"

"In the second one you do not clean anything on the way in. You take the raw data and you dump it straight into the warehouse exactly as it came. Ugly columns, strange values, all of it. And then you do the cleaning afterwards, inside the warehouse, using the warehouse's own power."

Uddhav looked at the two lines. They really were the same three words in a different order.

"So in the first one the transformation happens outside," he said, "and in the second one it happens inside."

"That is the difference. Now, what do you think you gain by doing it the second way?"

Uddhav thought about it properly this time, instead of reaching for a phrase.

"You still have the raw data," he said slowly. "In the first one, the messy version never gets stored anywhere. Only the cleaned version is kept. In the second one, the raw version is sitting right there in the warehouse forever."

Keshav pointed the pen at him.

"That is the answer. That is the sentence you did not have on Tuesday. Everything else follows from it." He turned the page. "Here is what it looks like on actual rows."

"Now six months from now somebody asks which coupons actually drive orders," said Keshav. "Under the first approach, that column was thrown away on the way through and never stored. The question cannot be answered at all. Under the second, the coupon has been sitting in the raw table the whole time and the answer is a query away."

"So it is not only about fixing mistakes," said Uddhav. "It is about questions nobody has thought of yet."

"Which is most of them."

"Follows how?"

"Think about what happens when your cleaning logic turns out to be wrong. Not if. When." Keshav leaned forward. "Say six months from now somebody notices that your revenue numbers have been quietly ignoring one type of discount this whole time. Six months of wrong reports. Now you have to fix it."

"I rewrite the transformation."

"Sure. And then what do you run it on?" Keshav waited. "In the first setup, the raw data was never kept. It was cleaned on the way through and thrown away. So to fix six months of history you have to go back and pull six months of data out of the source database all over again, assuming the source even still has it, which for old records it often does not."

"And in the second setup the raw data never left."

"It is still sitting in the warehouse where you dumped it. You write the corrected logic, run it over the raw data you already have, and by the afternoon your six months of history is correct. You never touch the source database at all."

Uddhav sat with that for a second. It was such a plain advantage that he was slightly annoyed he had never articulated it.

"So the raw data is a backup of the truth," he said.

"It is better than a backup. A backup is what you restore when something breaks. Raw data is what you rebuild from when your own thinking changes. And your thinking changes constantly, because the business changes constantly."

He wrote out the comparison properly.

Both do extract, transform and load. Only the position of the transform moves.

"Now the part you missed on Tuesday," said Keshav. "When would you not do it this way?"

"You said ELT is the right default."

"It is the right default. Defaults are not laws." He tapped the last row of the table. "Suppose the source has data you are not legally allowed to keep. Identity numbers, card details, medical records, whatever it is. Under ELT you would be loading all of that into your warehouse in its raw form and then cleaning it afterwards. Which means for some period of time it is sitting in your storage, in the clear."

"So you strip it out before it lands."

"You strip it out before it lands. Which is transform, then load. Which is ETL." Keshav shrugged. "There are a few others. Sometimes the data has to be reduced before it crosses a slow network. Sometimes the destination is an old system that cannot do transformations well. But sensitive data is the one an interviewer is most likely to have in mind."

Uddhav wrote it down. Choice, reason, and the case where it flips. He was starting to see how the three parts fitted together in practice, and it was less mysterious than it had felt on Tuesday.

"Say the whole thing," said Keshav. "Out loud. Do not write it, say it. Writing is easy. It was the saying that killed you."

Uddhav felt slightly foolish, sitting in his friend's living room talking to nobody. He said it anyway. He got halfway, lost the thread, and started again. The second time it came out cleanly.

"Better," said Keshav. "That is the first thing you have said all week that sounded like an engineer."

"You are enjoying this far too much."

"I told you the fee." He took the last samosa. "Same time next week. And bring a harder question."

Wrong "I would use ELT because it is the modern approach and it is what most teams do now."
Right "I would load the orders in raw and transform inside the warehouse, so that when the reporting logic changes we can rebuild history from data we already hold, without touching the source again. If the orders contained data we were not allowed to store, I would transform before loading instead."
The question, and how to hold it

"Would you use ETL or ELT here?"

Never answer with only one word. The interviewer is offering you an obvious opening to show trade-off reasoning, and taking the single word throws it away. Name the order you would choose, give the reason in terms of this situation, and then name the condition that would change your mind. Sensitive data is the usual reason to flip to ETL.

What Uddhav Learned

Every pipeline does three jobs: extract, transform and load. ETL and ELT are not different technologies. They are the same three jobs in a different order, and the only thing that moves is where the transformation happens.

ETL cleans the data before it is stored, so the warehouse only ever holds the tidy version. ELT stores the raw version first and cleans it inside the warehouse, so both versions exist. Keeping the raw version is what makes it possible to correct old mistakes and answer new questions later, by rerunning the logic on data you already have.

ELT is the sensible default for cloud systems. It stops being the default when the data must not be stored in its raw form, for example when it contains personal details that have to be removed before anything is written down.

The order is the decision. Clean before, and you keep only what you thought you needed. Clean after, and you keep the chance to change your mind.

Chapter Three

Why the World Changed Its Mind

Cheap storage and the freedom it bought

The Idea Old rules usually had good reasons. Learn the reason, and you will know when the rule has expired.

The following Thursday the monsoon arrived properly. Rain came straight down in sheets, loud on every roof in the lane, and Uddhav climbed three floors with his umbrella still dripping and his trousers wet to the knee. Keshav laughed at him for a full minute before producing a towel.

They shut the window, which made the room hot, and opened it again, which made the floor wet, and settled on a compromise of two inches. Rain drummed on the sheet-metal awning of the shop below. Somewhere in the building a power cut took out a television mid-sentence, and then gave it back.

"I have a question," Uddhav said, once he was dry and holding tea. "You said ELT is the default now. But you also said ETL was the default for decades. Were all those people wrong?"

Keshav looked genuinely pleased.

"That is a much better question than anything you asked me last week," he said. "And no. They were not wrong. They were correct for the world they were in. The world changed underneath them."

"Changed how?"

"Three things changed, and honestly the first one is most of it." He put down his cup. "Storage got cheap. Not a bit cheaper. Absurdly cheaper."

"When you had to buy the disks yourself and keep them in a room in your own building, storing data cost somewhere between one and five dollars per gigabyte every month. Sounds small. It is not small. Keep a terabyte of raw data for a year that way and the bill lands somewhere between twelve thousand and sixty thousand dollars."

Uddhav blinked. "For one terabyte."

"For one terabyte, per year, in the old world." Keshav wrote two numbers on the page. "Now look at cloud object storage today. Two point three cents per gigabyte per month for the standard tier. That same terabyte for a year costs two hundred and seventy six dollars. And if you move it to the cold archive tier, where you rarely read it, forty eight."

"Now think about what that does to the argument," Keshav said. "In the old world, every gigabyte you kept cost real money. So the sensible thing was to clean the data first, keep only the small tidy result, and throw the messy original away. That is not laziness. That is good engineering for those prices."

"And once storage costs almost nothing," said Uddhav, "throwing the original away stops saving you anything worth having."

"It saves you two hundred and seventy six dollars and costs you the ability to ever fix your history. That is a terrible trade. So the rule flipped." Keshav sat back. "That is the first change, and if you only remember one, remember that one."

"What are the other two?"

"The second is that storage and computing power came apart." He drew two boxes with a gap between them. "In the old world, the machine that held your data and the machine that did your thinking were the same machine, or at least permanently attached to each other. So you had a server sitting there running all day and all night, whether it was doing anything or not, and you paid for it either way."

"And now?"

"Now the data sits quietly in storage, costing almost nothing, and the computing power is something you rent by the minute. Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks, they all work this way. If your transformations take two hours a day, you pay for two hours a day. The other twenty two hours cost you nothing at all."

Uddhav saw the connection. "Which means doing the transformation inside the warehouse stopped being expensive."

"It stopped being expensive, and it stopped needing a permanent machine sitting in the middle of your pipeline waiting for work. Half the reason ETL existed was that staging server sitting in the middle. Once you can summon compute on demand, the staging server has no job left."

"And the third change?"

"The third one is less about money." Keshav thought for a moment. "Businesses started changing their minds much faster than they used to. New products, new questions, new definitions of things. What counted as an active user in January is not what counts in June."

"So the shape you decided up front stops fitting."

"Constantly. And under the old approach that hurt, because you had to decide the exact shape of everything before you were allowed to store it. Change the definition, rebuild the whole pipeline. Under the new approach the raw data is already there, so a new definition is just a new query written against data you already have."

Keshav wrote the three of them down in a row.

"Here is why I am making you learn the history," Keshav said, "and it is not so you can recite it at someone."

Uddhav waited.

"Anyone can memorise which approach is currently fashionable. That knowledge expires. What does not expire is knowing the reason underneath it." He tapped the table. "Every one of these three reasons could change again. Storage prices could move. Something new could tie compute back to storage. If you only learned the rule, you will not notice. If you learned the reason, you will see it coming."

"So when the interviewer asks why the industry moved to ELT," Uddhav said, "they are checking whether I understand the economics or just the fashion."

"They are checking exactly that. And it takes you about twenty seconds to show it. Storage got cheap, compute came apart from storage, and requirements keep changing. Three sentences. Say those and you sound like you have thought about it, because you will have."

The rain was still going outside. Uddhav noticed he had stopped dreading these evenings and started looking forward to them, which was a slightly strange thing to notice about being corrected for two hours a week.

Wrong "ETL is the old way and ELT is the new way. Everyone uses ELT now."
Right "ETL made sense when storage was expensive, because keeping only the cleaned result saved real money. Once cloud storage became cheap and compute could be rented separately, keeping the raw data stopped costing anything meaningful, and it bought the ability to reprocess history. That is what moved the default."
The question, and how to hold it

"Why did the industry move from ETL to ELT?"

Answer with economics, not with fashion. Cheap storage removed the reason to discard raw data. Separating storage from compute removed the need for a permanent transformation server. Constantly changing requirements made preserved raw data valuable. A rule you can justify is worth ten rules you have memorised.

What Uddhav Learned

The older approach was not a mistake. It was correct for a world where storing data was genuinely expensive, and where the machine that held your data was the same machine that processed it.

Three changes moved the default. Cloud storage became cheap enough that keeping raw data costs very little. Storage and compute separated, so processing power can be rented only while it is being used, which removed the need for a dedicated transformation server. And business requirements began changing so often that preserved raw data became more valuable than a tidy fixed schema.

The point of knowing this is not to recite it. It is that rules expire when their reasons expire. Learn the reason and you can tell when the rule no longer applies.

Learn why the rule exists, and you will know the day it stops being true.

Chapter Four

Five Stages

The shape under every pipeline

The Idea Every pipeline has the same five stages. Knowing them means you always know where to start talking.

The rain had stopped that afternoon and left the evening washed and cool, the first comfortable air in weeks. Somebody on the floor below had jasmine on their balcony, and with the window open the whole room smelled of it.

Uddhav had news, and he made himself wait until they were both sitting down before he said it.

"I have another interview. A payments company. Three weeks from Friday."

Keshav set the plate of samosas down on the low table and looked at him properly for the first time that evening.

"Three weeks is enough," he said. "Barely. How do you feel?"

"Sick, mostly."

"Good. That is the correct amount." He pulled the notebook across the table. "Then we stop wandering about and get organised, because the reason you froze last time was not only that you had no reasons. It was that you had no order to say them in."

"When someone says design me a pipeline, your head goes blank because the question is enormous," Keshav went on. "It could mean anything. So the first thing you need is not knowledge. It is a place to stand."

"A place to stand."

"A fixed order you walk through every time, no matter what the question is. Every pipeline that has ever been built does five things, in the same order. Learn the five and you will never be lost at the start of an answer again."

He wrote them down the side of the page.

1. Ingest     get the data out of the source
2. Store      put it somewhere durable and organised
3. Transform  clean it, join it, aggregate it
4. Serve      make it available to whoever needs it
5. Monitor    know when any of the above breaks

"That is it?" said Uddhav.

"That is it. Every pipeline. The ones you built, the ones I build, the ones at companies with a thousand engineers. Same five." Keshav shrugged. "The difficulty is never the list. The difficulty is that each stage hides a decision, and the interviewer is waiting to see whether you know which decision lives where."

"Go through them."

"Ingest first. This is where the data enters your world, and there are four questions here worth asking out loud." He counted them off. "Does your pipeline go and fetch the data, or does the source push it to you? Do you collect it on a schedule or continuously? Do you take everything every time, or only what changed? And do you check the shape of the data on the way in, or let anything through and check later?"

"What does that last one actually mean in practice?"

"It has two names worth knowing. Checking on the way in is called schema on write. You decide what a valid row looks like, and anything that fails is rejected at the door. You find out about problems immediately, which is good, but you also throw away data that might have been useful."

"And the other one?"

"Schema on read. You accept everything, however strange, and you work out what it means later when you actually read it. Nothing is ever lost, but the problems move downstream and you find out about them further away from the cause."

"Say an app release starts sending a signup event with the age as text instead of a number, and no country at all." He wrote the offending record down. "What should happen?"

"And if you are strict at the door with nothing behind it, a bad release on Friday means signups you never see again. That is a genuinely bad Monday."

"The last one sounds like it matters a lot," said Uddhav.

"It matters enough that we are spending a whole evening on it in two weeks. Leave it for now. Just know it belongs to ingest."

"Four decisions."

"What kind of store. What format the files are written in. How the data is split up. And whether you keep one copy or several at different levels of cleanliness." He wrote them. "And format?"

"If your data is going to be read column by column for reports, you want a format that stores it column by column, so a query reading three columns out of twenty only touches those three. "And splitting it up?"

"Split it along whatever you filter by most, which is almost always the date. If your data is stored in folders by date, a query asking about last Tuesday reads one folder instead of three years of history." He paused. "We do a whole evening on this later, so I will not go further tonight."

"This is worth knowing by its nickname, because you will hear people say it. You keep the data in layers. The raw layer, exactly as it arrived, untouched. Then a cleaned layer, where the mess has been fixed and you would be happy for someone to use it. Then a final layer, shaped for the specific reports people actually run."

"And the nickname?"

"Bronze, silver and gold. Raw, cleaned, ready." Keshav grinned. "It sounds like a medal ceremony, and it is genuinely how people talk. The reason it exists is that when something is wrong, you can go back to the layer below and rebuild upward, rather than going all the way to the source."

"Whether you do it in one pass or in layers. Layers are more work to set up, and they let you rebuild one level without redoing everything, which pays for itself the first time something is wrong."

"Serve is where people forget that pipelines exist for somebody. Data that nobody can read is not finished, it is just stored." He pointed the pen. "And who reads it changes what you build. "That is a little harsh."

Where Data Comes From

Matching the method to the source

The Idea Each kind of source has one method that suits it naturally. Name the source, and the method follows.

Copying the entire dataset every run is simple, has no moving parts, and handles deletes automatically because the old copy is replaced. It stops being viable as tables grow, because the cost is paid on the whole table even when almost nothing has changed.

Copying only what changed is dramatically cheaper, but it requires a way to know what changed, and there are four, with very different completeness. A last-updated column is simple but depends on the application maintaining it, and cannot see deletes. An ever-increasing identifier only works for data that is never modified. Reading the database's change log captures inserts, updates and deletes, and is the most complete method. Comparing row fingerprints also catches everything but requires reading the whole table each run.

The deciding question is size and rate of change, asked before choosing. The deciding detail, once incremental is chosen, is what happens to deleted rows.

A deleted row cannot be found by asking what changed. It is gone. Only the log saw it leave.

Four strategies reduce it. Broadcast a small table to every worker so the large one never moves, which suits the very common pattern of a huge table of events joined to a small table describing them. And check whether the expensive operation is needed at all, since deduplication in particular is often better solved when the data arrives.

Skew is the other shuffle failure. Because every record for a key lands on one task, an unevenly spread key gives one task far more work than the rest, and the job takes as long as its slowest task. Nine hundred and ninety nine tasks finishing in two minutes does not help if the thousandth takes two hours. Adding machines cannot fix it, because the problem is one key rather than a shortage of workers.

The remedy is salting. Add a random suffix to the busy key so it spreads across several tasks, then run a second small pass that strips the suffix and combines the partial results. The second pass handles only a handful of rows and costs almost nothing.

If the two sides are wildly different sizes, only one of them should be moving. And if one key is far bigger than the rest, split it.

He did not laugh, which he was fairly pleased about, because somebody had once not laughed at him.

"Your answer was correct," he said. "Incremental is right for a table that size. If they only wanted the answer you passed in the first ten minutes."

"Then why did I lose?"

"Because that was not the question they were asking." He reached for a sheet of paper, because there were no napkins. "Sit down. I want to show you three words."

What Uddhav Learned

Nothing in the interview was a question he had not already been asked on a Thursday. The sizing came first, the requirements were separated by their deadlines, the expensive component was justified in money rather than importance, the grain was stated in a sentence, and the change to the area dimension was handled as history rather than an update.

None of that was recalled. It had become the shape his thinking took, which is the only durable outcome of preparation. Facts are forgotten; a habit of reasoning is not.

He also changed an answer mid-interview, out loud, and explained why the second one was better. That is not a failure to be hidden. Choosing the finer grain because detail cannot be recovered is exactly the reasoning being tested.

And the last thing he learned was not technical at all. The three words came from a napkin, and the napkin came from somebody who had once sat where he was sitting, and had been taught by somebody else. Nobody in that line was paid for it. It simply moved forward.

They are not asking what you know. They are asking how you decide. And once you can say why, you can teach somebody else to.

The whole of Part One, in one page

Decide where transformation happens. Keeping raw data is what buys you the ability to fix history, which is why ELT is the default and sensitive data is the usual reason to flip.

Walk the five stages when a question is too big: ingest, store, transform, serve, monitor. Match the ingestion method to the source type. Read the change log when deletes matter.

Size before choosing. Copy everything while it is small, copy only changes once it is not, and always say what happens to deleted rows. Assume every job runs twice, so replace rather than append, take the date as a parameter, and keep the raw layer so replays never touch the source.

Choice, reason, and the case where it flips. Everything else is detail.
